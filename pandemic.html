Stopping a pandemic

Good respirators could stop even a worst-case pandemic

If everyone who has to keep working wore a well-fitting respirator, and everyone else stayed home for a while, even a very contagious, very deadly airborne virus would stop spreading. That buys time for vaccines.

This page explains the argument in “Universal respirator usage stops worst-case pandemics” by Constantin Arnscheidt and Damon Binder (Defenses in Depth, October 2026), with workforce and supply figures from two RAND reports.

1COVID-19 was bad. It was not the worst case.

The original COVID-19 virus killed roughly 1 in 200 of the people it infected. SARS in 2003 killed about 1 in 10, but it was contained after fewer than 10,000 cases. Smallpox was both contagious and deadly: its first epidemics may have killed about a third of the population in some places.

The authors worry that advances in biotechnology and AI could make it easier to engineer a virus that is both. Preventing that matters most, but it is worth having a plan if it happens anyway.

Vaccines take months to develop and make at scale. Respirators can hold the line until then, because they work against any airborne virus and can be made and stored in advance.

2Push R below 1 and the outbreak dies out

R is how many people, on average, each infected person goes on to infect. Above 1, cases multiply each generation. Below 1, the outbreak fizzles out. With nobody immune or protected, it is roughly 1.5 to 6.5 for the original COVID-19 virus, 3.5 to 6 for smallpox and 12 to 18 for measles.

A mask cuts R twice: it stops some of what you breathe in, and if the infected person wears one too, some of what they breathe out.

Everyone in a mask of protection factor PF cuts R by PF × PF

That is why universal wearing is so powerful: a group protection factor of 18, worn by everyone, cuts transmission about 324 times.

3Most people stay home. Essential workers mask up.

An airborne virus has to get from one person to another through shared air. If every household kept to itself, it would stop. But someone has to keep the power on, move food and medicine, run hospitals and make the vaccines. The authors’ “minimum viable” plan:

  1. As soon as a dangerous new virus is detected, people who don’t need to go to work stay home.
  2. Essential workers keep working, in good respirators whenever they share air, so that R among them stays below 1.
  3. This holds until vaccines, treatments or better tests arrive.

US estimates of essential workers range from about 10 to 30 million. RAND went occupation by occupation, because risk differs a lot within a sector: a hospital nurse and a hospital accountant face very different exposure. About 39% of critical-sector workers could work from home.

4The worst case is probably airborne

A virus that spreads fast enough to be a worst case will most likely spread through the air: it is much harder to avoid breathing shared air than to avoid contaminated food, touching people or mosquito bites.

People breathe out particles of all sizes. Big ones fall within a few metres; small ones drift across a room for much longer. COVID-19, flu, colds and measles all spread substantially through the air, and smallpox probably did too. Block airborne spread and the other routes are easier to deal with.

5How respirators get there

A protection factor is how many times less you breathe in. Two things limit it:

  • Leaks round the edges. If 1 in 20 breaths of air slips past the seal, the mask can never do better than 20×, however good the filter.
  • Time off. A mask worn 95% of the time in shared air can never do better than 20×, because the other 5% gets in unfiltered.

Official ratings, the ones the rest of this guide uses, are deliberately cautious. Studies of groups wearing respirators through a working day usually find more. What matters for stopping spread is the group’s average, worked out so that a few badly fitting masks pull it down a lot.

Exhalation valves give up part of the benefit. A valve lets some of the wearer’s breath out unfiltered, weakening the “infected person wears one too” half of PF × PF. Pick a mask without a valve, or fit a filter over it.

6How much protection is enough?

The authors built a deliberately pessimistic model: an infectious worker at their most contagious, sharing an office for a 50-hour week. Because everyone wears a mask, the protection factor needed is the square root of how contagious the virus could be in that setting.

Even the most extreme case needs about 64×, which a well-fitted reusable respirator can reach. Protection factors below 18 could have stopped the original COVID-19 virus, if everyone had worn them consistently. Wearing them consistently is the hard part.

7Air cleaning helps, but can’t do it alone

Realistic ventilation and filter upgrades cut what you breathe in by up to about 5×. Far-UVC lamps, which kill viruses in the air, might reach 20–40× within safe light levels. Glycol vapours look modest.

Ventilation alone can’t close the gap: a 900× cut would mean replacing all the air in a room 900 times an hour, far beyond even an operating theatre. But cleaner air stacks with masks: a 4× cut brings the protection factor needed from 30× down to about 15×.

8A stockpile would be cheap

Disposable respirators cost about $1 each at bulk prices and last a few days. A reusable half mask costs about $30 and lasts years, so past about three months it is cheaper, and it protects better. That makes half masks with P100 filters the authors’ pick for a stockpile.

For the US, 20 million would cost about $600 million, plus about as much again for storage and handing them out. Spread over a 20-year stockpile, that is roughly $60 million a year: under 0.001% of federal spending.

9Why not just make them when we need them?

Because when a pandemic starts, everyone needs them at once. RAND looked at why the US struggled to make enough respirators during COVID-19:

  • Most masks are made in one place. Before COVID, China made about half the world’s masks, around 20 million a day. During the pandemic it supplied over 85% of US imports of N95s and face masks, and export restrictions can cut that off.
  • The filter fabric is a bottleneck. N95s rely on a special “meltblown” polypropylene fabric, and the material and its machines run short exactly when demand surges.
  • New makers don’t survive the quiet years. Of 107 US protective equipment companies started during the pandemic, 102 have stopped operating, undercut by cheaper imports.

RAND’s conclusion: stockpiles are essential but not enough on their own. They need a stable domestic manufacturing base behind them, which needs predictable demand such as long-term contracts. Source: RAND (2026).

10What it means if you’re choosing a mask

A reusable half mask is the sweet spot

With P100 or P3 filters it protects more than a disposable respirator, and over months it costs less.

Fit matters more than the filter

Most of what gets in comes round the edges. Use head straps rather than ear loops, shape the nose clip with both hands, and get fit tested if you can.

Wear it whenever you share air

Ten minutes off counts for as much as 3 hours 20 minutes in a 20× mask. Consistency is what drives R below 1.

Skip the valve if you might be ill

A mask without an exhalation valve protects the people around you too, which is half of the PF × PF effect.

Watch out for fakes. The CDC estimates that about 60% of KN95s in the US are fake, and from May 2025 to January 2026 NIOSH identified 1,245 fraudulent online listings misusing its approval marks. Buy from the maker or a known distributor, and check an N95’s approval number on NIOSH’s Certified Equipment List.

What this assumes, and sources

The authors’ model is deliberately pessimistic and isn’t fitted to any one disease. It assumes evenly mixed air, no decay of the virus in the air, and that visibly ill workers stay home. The tools on this page are simplified from it.

The measured protection factors come from small studies, mostly of trained workers, and reaching 18× across millions of people depends on wearing masks consistently. The costs leave out training, logistics and the cost of people staying home.
